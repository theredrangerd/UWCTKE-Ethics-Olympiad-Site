What is the UWC-TKEthics Olympiad?

Forget everything you know about debate. The UWC-TKEthics Olympiad brings high school students from schools around the world together to wrestle with the ethical dilemmas that don't have clean answers — and instead of fighting to win, teams are rewarded for thinking clearly, listening well, and changing their minds when the argument earns it. Organized by the Model Philosophy Ethics Olympiad (MPEO) leadership team, it's not about who lands the sharpest rebuttal; it's about who reasons with the most honesty.

Expect a room — well, a Zoom — full of sharp, curious students from different countries and school systems going head-to-head with ideas instead of insults, building the kind of critical thinking, philosophical range, and cross-cultural fluency a normal debate tournament never asks for.

How the competition works

Anatomy of a round

Unlike a combative debate, an Ethics Olympiad round unfolds in four civil, collaborative stages:

01

Ethical Presentation 5 min

Team A opens with a structured stance on an unannounced ethical case — naming the moral tension at its core, identifying the stakeholders involved, and applying a philosophical lens (utilitarian, deontological, virtue-based) to make their case.

02

Collaborative Commentary 2 min

The opposing team responds — not with a rebuttal, but with constructive commentary: what's the blind spot, what's been left out, where does the case deserve more nuance.

03

Response 3 min

The presenting team addresses that commentary head-on — sharpening, defending, or revising their position in real time.

04

Judges' Questions

Professional ethicists probe the case further, testing for clarity, rigor, and intellectual honesty — this is where the strongest teams actually stand out.

Teams then swap roles for a second case, and the process repeats across four rounds in total.

This is not a debate. Teams can agree with each other's position. You are not scored on winning an argument — you're scored on how clearly, honestly, and thoughtfully you engage with the ethical dimensions of the case.

Preparing for a round

You do not need a philosophy background to compete. Case packages are sent five weeks before the event, and the resources below cover the ethical frameworks, judging rubric, and case archive you'll want to work through beforehand:

  • Prep resource folder — case archives, primers, and practice materials for registered teams.
  • Modus Ponens Institute resources — the judging rubric, ethical framework guides, and coaching materials from our judging partner.

FAQ

Is this a debate?

No. Teams can agree with each other's position — you're judged on the quality of your reasoning, not on winning an argument.

Do I need to know ethical theory beforehand?

No prior experience is required. Read the case package carefully, think through the arguments, and you're ready — see the "How the competition works" section above for a primer.

What are the microphone/conferencing rules on the day?

Keep your microphone muted unless invited to speak by the moderator/judge. During your team's private conference time, you may turn your mic off if everyone is in one room; if your team is spread across locations, use a separate call for team discussion and stay silent on it outside your allotted conference times.

What happens if there's a tie?

The head judge breaks ties using the highest score in the first heat; if that's still tied, the highest score in the second heat, and so on.

How do I challenge a result?

Complaints or challenges to results must be submitted in writing to the organizers. Results are otherwise final and are published within 24 hours of the competition.

Can I use outside research to prepare?

Yes — but simply presenting facts won't score well on its own. You need valid, persuasive arguments backed by facts, and if you cite a specific fact not in the case itself, name your source (e.g. "according to a 2011 article in National Geographic…").
